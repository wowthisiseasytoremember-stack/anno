## 1. Executive Summary & Historical-Theological Foundations

The Catholic experience in East Asia occupies a unique place in the history of global Christianity. Unlike the Mediterranean basin, where the Church expanded along established Roman trade routes, or Latin America, where evangelization was closely tied to colonial expansion, Catholicism in Japan and Korea developed amidst extreme isolation, intense political scrutiny, and severe state-sponsored persecution. The sacred corridor connecting Nagasaki and Seoul bears witness to centuries of heroic witness, underground faith, and resilience.

In Japan, the faith was introduced in 1549 by Saint Francis Xavier and grew rapidly during the Sengoku period, only to face total prohibition under Toyotomi Hideyoshi and the Tokugawa Shogunate. The resulting persecution aimed not only at execution, but at the systematic eradication of Christian memory through mandatory apostasy tests (*fumi-e*), psychological torture, and communal surveillance. Despite the complete expulsion or martyrdom of all clergy by the mid-seventeenth century, the faith survived underground for more than 250 years through the *Kakure Kirishitan* (Hidden Christians).

Conversely, the Church in Korea was established through a lay intellectual movement. In 1784, Confucian scholars seeking truth encountered Christian doctrinal treatises translated into Classical Chinese and acquired in Beijing. Yi Seung-hun received baptism in Beijing, returned to Joseon, and baptized his fellow scholars, founding an active ecclesial community years before the arrival of the first foreign priest. This triggered successive nationwide purges—the Shinyu (1801), Gihae (1839), Byeongo (1846), and Byeongin (1866) persecutions—resulting in more than 10,000 martyrs.

## 3. Detailed Hagiographical Analyses

### Nishizaka Hill: The First Japanese Golgotha
On February 5, 1597, twenty-six Catholics were crucified on this ridge overlooking Nagasaki Bay. The group included six European Franciscan friars, three Japanese Jesuit scholastics and catechists, and seventeen Japanese laymen, among them three young altar boys: Louis Ibaraki (age 12), Antony Deynan (age 13), and Thomas Kozaki (age 14). Arrested in Kyoto and Osaka following the San Felipe incident, the martyrs endured mutilation—having their left earlobes severed—and were forced to march more than 800 kilometers through freezing winter conditions to Nagasaki. From his cross, Saint Paul Miki proclaimed his samurai lineage and his complete forgiveness of his executioners.

### Oura Basilica: The Reemergence of the Hidden Christians
Constructed in 1864 by French Paris Foreign Missions Society (M.E.P.) priests, Fathers Louis Furet and Bernard Petitjean, Oura Church was dedicated to the Twenty-Six Martyrs canonized by Pope Pius IX in 1862. On March 17, 1865, a group of local farmers and fishermen from Urakami entered the church. A woman named Yuri Sugimoto approached Father Petitjean and whispered: *"All of us have the same heart as you... Where is the statue of Santa Maria?"* Pope Pius IX characterized this survival as the "Miracle of the Orient."

### Urakami Cathedral: Purification and Resurrection
Following the discovery at Oura, the Meiji administration launched the *Urakami Yoban Kuzure* (Fourth Persecution) in 1867, exiling over 3,400 villagers to labor camps until 1873. Over three decades, they constructed Urakami Cathedral brick by brick on the site of the magistrate's office where their ancestors were forced to tread on *fumi-e*. On August 9, 1945, the atomic bomb detonated 500 meters from the cathedral walls, killing approximately 8,500 Catholic parishioners. The charred wooden head of the Immaculate Conception statue (*Hibaku Mary*) was salvaged and stands as a global symbol of peace and spiritual rebirth.

### Jeoldusan: The Lay Confessors' Bluff
Occupying a promontory over the Han River historically known as *Jamdubong* ("Silkworm Head Hill"), Jeoldusan became an execution site during the Byeongin Persecution of 1866. Renamed *Jeoldusan* ("Beheading Mountain"), the promontory saw thousands of lay Catholics systematically beheaded or strangled with weighted torture stones, their bodies cast into the river. The shrine houses a subterranean crypt holding the relics of twenty-eight martyrs.

### Saenamteo: The Sandbank of Clerical Martyrdom
Saenamteo, an open sandy expanse along the northern bank of the Han River, served as an official Joseon military training ground and execution site for high treason. Eleven of the fourteen priest-martyrs in Korean Catholic history were executed here. In 1839, Bishop Laurent Imbert, Fr. Pierre Maubant, and Fr. Jacques Chastan surrendered themselves to prevent further massacres of their flock. On September 16, 1846, Saint Andrew Kim Tae-gon, the first native Korean priest, was martyred here at age 25.
